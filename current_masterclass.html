1 Charge, Current & Potential Difference

To master current electricity, we must start at the microscopic level. Everything in this module is dictated by the movement of electrons. In a solid metallic conductor, the atoms are fixed in a lattice, but valence electrons are loosely bound and free to move randomly. When an external force is applied, these electrons undergo a slow, collective drift.

1. Electric Charge ($Q$) & Quantization

Electric charge is an intrinsic property of fundamental particles. It comes in two flavors: positive (protons) and negative (electrons). The most important property of charge for ICSE is its Quantization.

Total charge ($Q$) on a body is always an integral multiple of the basic quantum of charge ($e$), which is the charge on a single electron.

$$ Q = ne $$
  • $n$ = an integer ($1, 2, 3...$)
  • $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$
  • SI Unit of Charge: Coulomb (C)

Electric Current ($I$)

The rate of flow of electric charge through a cross-section of a conductor.

$$ I = \frac{Q}{t} = \frac{ne}{t} $$
Define 1 Ampere: Current is said to be 1 Ampere if 1 Coulomb of charge flows through a cross-section in 1 second.

Potential Difference ($V$)

The amount of work done in moving a unit positive charge from one point to another against the electric field.

$$ V = \frac{W}{Q} $$
Define 1 Volt: The potential difference between two points is 1 Volt if 1 Joule of work is done in moving 1 Coulomb of charge between them.

Crucial Concept: Conventional vs. Electronic Current

In an external circuit, electrons (negative charge) physically drift from the negative terminal to the positive terminal. They are repelled by the negative pole and attracted by the positive pole.

However, conventional current is historically defined as the direction a positive charge would move. Therefore, conventional current flows from the Positive terminal to the Negative terminal. Always draw circuit arrows pointing from $+$ to $-$.

2 Ohm's Law, Resistance & Conductance

German physicist Georg Simon Ohm discovered the fundamental relationship connecting current and potential difference in 1826. It remains the bedrock of electrical circuit analysis.

Statement of Ohm's Law

The electric current flowing through a metallic wire is directly proportional to the potential difference applied across its ends, provided its temperature and other physical conditions (like mechanical strain) remain constant.

Note: Omitting the bolded text in the exam results in zero marks.

Mathematically, $ V \propto I $. Introducing a constant of proportionality:

$$ V = IR $$

Where $R$ is the Resistance of the conductor. Resistance is the obstruction offered to the flow of current by the collisions of drifting electrons with the fixed positive ions of the metal lattice.

Factors Affecting Resistance of a Conductor

1
Length ($l$)

Resistance is directly proportional to length. ($R \propto l$). A longer wire offers more resistance because electrons suffer more collisions.

2
Area of Cross-Section ($A$)

Resistance is inversely proportional to area. ($R \propto 1/A$). A thicker wire provides a wider path, meaning fewer collisions and less resistance.

3
Temperature

For pure metals, resistance increases with an increase in temperature, as the lattice ions vibrate more vigorously, impeding electron flow.

4
Material

Depends entirely on the free electron density ($n$) of the specific substance. Copper has high $n$, hence very low resistance.

Conductance ($G$)

The reciprocal of resistance is called Conductance. $G = 1/R$. Its SI unit is $Ohm^{-1}$, also known as Siemens (S) or historically, mho ($\mho$).

Ohmic vs. Non-Ohmic Resistors

Feature Ohmic Conductors Non-Ohmic Conductors
Obedience Strictly obey Ohm's law. $V/I$ is constant. Do not obey Ohm's law. $V/I$ varies.
V-I Graph A straight line passing through the origin. A curve.
Dynamic Resistance Remains exactly the same at all voltages. Changes continuously with voltage/current.
Examples Silver, Copper, Nichrome, Dilute Sulphuric Acid. LED, Junction Diode, Solar Cell, Filament of a bulb.

3 Specific Resistance (Resistivity)

While Resistance ($R$) depends on the dimensions (length and area) of the wire, we need a metric that tells us about the innate electrical quality of the material itself, regardless of its shape. This is Specific Resistance.

Combining the factors of length and area, we know $ R \propto \frac{l}{A} $. To remove the proportionality sign, we introduce a constant ($\rho$, rho).

Mathematical Formulation

$$ R = \rho \frac{l}{A} $$

$$ \rho = \frac{RA}{l} $$

SI Unit of Resistivity ($\rho$)

Ohm-metre ($\Omega \cdot \text{m}$)

Formal Definition:

The specific resistance of a material is the resistance of a wire of that material having unit length ($1\text{m}$) and unit area of cross-section ($1\text{m}^2$).

The Ultimate ICSE Trap: Stretching a Wire

Question: A copper wire is stretched until its length is doubled. What happens to its (a) Resistance, and (b) Specific Resistance?

Part (a) Resistance: It becomes 4 times ($4R$)

Derivation: When you stretch a wire, its Volume remains constant ($V = Al$). If length is doubled ($l' = 2l$), the area must be halved ($A' = A/2$) to maintain the volume.
New Resistance $R' = \rho \frac{l'}{A'} = \rho \frac{2l}{(A/2)} = 4 \left(\rho \frac{l}{A}\right) = 4R$.
Shortcut Formula: If a wire is stretched to $n$ times its length, $R' = n^2 R$.

Part (b) Specific Resistance: Remains completely UNCHANGED.

Resistivity is a characteristic property of the material. Stretching copper leaves it as copper. It does not depend on shape, length, or area. It only depends on the material and temperature.

Choice of Materials (High Yield Exam Topic)

Connecting Wires

Copper or Aluminium. They have extremely low specific resistance (high conductivity), meaning they offer negligible resistance to current flow, preventing voltage drop and heat loss.

Heating Elements

Nichrome (Alloy of Ni, Cr, Mn, Fe). It has high specific resistance (produces a lot of heat, $H=I^2Rt$) and a high melting point. Crucially, it does not easily oxidize (burn) at high temperatures.

Standard Resistors

Constantan or Manganin. Their specific resistance is high, but more importantly, their temperature coefficient of resistance is almost zero. Their resistance does not fluctuate when the temperature changes during experiments.

4 EMF, Terminal Voltage & Internal Resistance

Every battery or cell is a chemical pump for electrons. However, the chemicals inside the cell (the electrolyte) are not perfect conductors; they offer a slight resistance to the flow of ions. This is called Internal Resistance ($r$).

1

Electromotive Force (EMF, $E$)

The work done by the cell in forcing a unit positive charge to flow through the complete circuit (both outside and inside the cell).

  • It is measured when the circuit is OPEN (no current is drawn).
  • It is a characteristic of the cell and depends entirely on the nature of electrodes and electrolyte.
  • It does not depend on the shape or size of the electrodes.
2

Terminal Voltage ($V$)

The work done by the cell in forcing a unit positive charge to flow across the external circuit only.

  • It is measured when the circuit is CLOSED (current is being drawn).
  • It depends on the external resistance connected to the cell.
  • When current is drawn, $V$ is always less than $E$.
Core Numerical Concept

The Complete Circuit Equation

The total energy supplied by the battery (EMF) is spent in two ways: doing work in the external circuit ($V$) and overcoming internal resistance within the battery itself ($v$).

Law of Conservation of Energy

$$ E = V + v $$

Main Circuit Current

$$ I = \frac{E}{R + r} $$

Voltage Drop ($v$): This is the "lost volts". It is the potential drop inside the cell due to its internal resistance. $ v = Ir $.

Terminal Voltage Formula: Since $E = V + Ir$, we can calculate terminal voltage as $V = E - Ir$.

Factors Affecting Internal Resistance ($r$) of a Cell:

  1. Surface area of electrodes: Larger area = less internal resistance.
  2. Distance between electrodes: Greater distance = more internal resistance.
  3. Concentration of electrolyte: Higher concentration = more internal resistance.
  4. Temperature: Higher temperature = less internal resistance (because electrolyte viscosity decreases).

5 Resistors in Series & Parallel

When multiple resistors are connected in a circuit, we can replace them with a single "Equivalent Resistor" that draws the exact same total current from the battery. The method of calculating this depends on how they are chained.

Series Circuit

Resistors are connected end-to-end. There is only a single, unbroken path for the flow of current.

Defining Traits:

  • Current ($I$) is CONSTANT through all resistors. ($I = I_1 = I_2$)
  • Voltage ($V$) DIVIDES across each resistor in direct proportion to their resistance.

Derivation of Equivalent Resistance

Total V = $V_1 + V_2 + V_3$

Using Ohm's Law ($V=IR$):

$IR_s = IR_1 + IR_2 + IR_3$

Canceling $I$ from both sides:

Final Formula

$$ R_s = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 $$

Result: $R_s$ is always greater than the largest individual resistance.

Parallel Circuit

One end of all resistors is connected to a common node, and the other ends to another common node.

Defining Traits:

  • Voltage ($V$) is CONSTANT across all branches. ($V = V_1 = V_2$)
  • Current ($I$) DIVIDES across branches in inverse proportion to their resistance.

Derivation of Equivalent Resistance

Total I = $I_1 + I_2 + I_3$

Using Ohm's Law ($I=V/R$):

$\frac{V}{R_p} = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \frac{V}{R_3}$

Canceling $V$ from both sides:

Final Formula

$$ \frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} $$

Result: $R_p$ is always smaller than the smallest individual resistance.

6 Electrical Energy, Power & Heating

When an electric current flows through a wire with resistance, the electrons continuously collide with the lattice ions, transferring their kinetic energy. This energy manifests as heat. This is known as Joule's Heating Effect.

Electrical Work (Energy)

Joule (J)

Derived from the definition of Potential Difference ($W = QV$) and Current ($Q = It$):

General Formula

$$ W = V I t $$

$$ W = I^2 R t $$

Use in Series

$$ W = \frac{V^2 t}{R} $$

Use in Parallel

Electrical Power

Watt (W)

Power is the rate at which electrical energy is consumed. ($P = W/t$). Just remove 't' from the energy formulas!

General Formula

$$ P = V I $$

$$ P = I^2 R $$

Use in Series

$$ P = \frac{V^2}{R} $$

Use in Parallel

Commercial Unit of Energy

The Joule is a very small unit of energy. For heavy household consumption, the electricity board bills you in "Units", scientifically known as the Kilowatt-hour (kWh). One kWh is the electrical energy consumed by an appliance of power 1kW when used for 1 hour.

Conversion to Joules

$1 \text{ kWh} = 1000 \text{ W} \times 3600 \text{ s}$

$$ 3.6 \times 10^6 \text{ J} $$

Decoding Appliance Power Ratings

If a bulb is rated "220V, 100W", it means if it is connected to a 220V supply, it will consume 100 Joules of energy per second. From this rating, you can always extract two absolute properties of the appliance:

1. Safe Current Limit ($I$)

The maximum current it can handle before the filament burns out.

$$ I = \frac{P}{V} $$

2. Resistance ($R$)

The fixed resistance of its internal filament/coil when hot.

$$ R = \frac{V^2}{P} $$

High Yield Board Material

Final Exam Trap Checklist

Circuit numericals are notorious for hiding small details that completely alter the result. Double-check these before submitting your paper:

The Voltmeter vs. Ammeter Placement

An Ammeter has virtually zero ideal resistance and must ALWAYS be connected in series. A Voltmeter has infinite ideal resistance and must ALWAYS be connected in parallel. Reversing them will short-circuit your drawing and yield zero marks.

Ignoring the tiny "$r$" in the battery

In circuit diagram numericals, always check the battery label. If it says "$12V, 2\Omega$", that $2\Omega$ is the internal resistance. You MUST add this to the total external equivalent resistance before calculating the main circuit current: $I = \frac{E}{R_{eq} + r}$.

The V-I Graph Axis Flip

Do not blindly assume the slope of a graph is Resistance. Look at the axes!
If $V$ is on the Y-axis and $I$ is on the X-axis: Slope = $V/I = R$ (Resistance).
If $I$ is on the Y-axis and $V$ is on the X-axis: Slope = $I/V = 1/R$ (Conductance). A steeper line here means lower resistance!

Which Bulb Glows Brighter?

In Series: Current ($I$) is constant. Use $P = I^2R$. The bulb with the higher resistance (lower rated power) dissipates more actual power and glows brighter.
In Parallel: Voltage ($V$) is constant. Use $P = V^2/R$. The bulb with the lower resistance (higher rated power) dissipates more actual power and glows brighter.
