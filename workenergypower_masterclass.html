1 The Physics of Work & Trigonometry

In everyday language, reading a book or thinking hard is considered "work". In Physics, this is absolutely false. For work to be done, two strict conditions must be satisfied: a force must act on the body, and the point of application of the force must be displaced in the direction of the force.

ICSE Definition of Work

Work is said to be done only when the force applied on a body makes the body move (i.e., there is a displacement of the point of application of force).

Note: Omitting "of the point of application" can lead to mark deductions.

The General Formula for Work

Work is the dot product of Force and Displacement vectors.

$$ W = F \cdot S \cdot \cos \theta $$

Where $\theta$ is the angle between the direction of force ($F$) and the direction of displacement ($S$).

The Three Cases of Work

Case 1: $\theta = 0^\circ$

Positive Work

Force and displacement are in the same direction. $\cos 0^\circ = 1$.

$$ W = F \times S $$

Example: A horse pulling a cart on a level road; A body falling freely under gravity.

Case 2: $\theta = 180^\circ$

Negative Work

Force acts in the opposite direction of displacement. $\cos 180^\circ = -1$.

$$ W = -(F \times S) $$

Example: Work done by friction on a sliding block; Throwing a ball vertically upwards (work done by gravity).

Case 3: $\theta = 90^\circ$

Zero Work

Force is perpendicular to displacement. $\cos 90^\circ = 0$.

$$ W = 0 $$

Example: A coolie carrying a load walking on a horizontal platform; Work done by centripetal force.

Units of Work

SI Unit: Joule (J). $1 \text{ J} = 1 \text{ N} \times 1 \text{ m}$. Work is said to be 1 Joule when a force of 1 Newton moves a body through a distance of 1 metre in its own direction.

CGS Unit: erg. $1 \text{ erg} = 1 \text{ dyne} \times 1 \text{ cm}$.

Relation: $1 \text{ Joule} = 10^7 \text{ ergs}$

2 Power: The Rate of Energy Transfer

If Person A and Person B both carry 50 kg of bricks up a flight of stairs, they do the exact same amount of work. However, if Person A does it in 1 minute and Person B takes 10 minutes, Person A is more powerful. Power incorporates time.

Power Formulas

Power is the rate of doing work, or the rate at which energy is supplied/consumed.

$$ P = \frac{W}{t} $$

In terms of Force & Velocity

$$ P = \frac{F \times S}{t} \implies P = F \times v $$

Units of Power

  • SI Unit: Watt (W) = 1 Joule/sec
  • Kilowatt: 1 kW = 1,000 W
  • Megawatt: 1 MW = $10^6$ W
  • Horsepower (H.P.):
    1 H.P. = 746 Watts

3 Energy Fundamentals & Potential Energy

The energy of a body is its capacity to do work. Energy is a scalar quantity. Its units are exactly the same as work (Joules, ergs). The total Mechanical Energy of a system is the sum of its Kinetic and Potential energy.

Potential Energy ($U$)

The energy possessed by a body by virtue of its specific position or changed configuration. It comes in two primary forms in classical mechanics:

Gravitational Potential Energy

Energy possessed by a body due to its position above the ground. The work done in lifting a body of mass $m$ to a height $h$ against gravity ($g$) gets stored as GPE.

$$ U = mgh $$

Elastic Potential Energy

Energy possessed by a body in a deformed state due to a change in its size or shape (configuration). Work is done against the restoring forces.

Example: A wound up watch spring; A stretched bowstring; A compressed catapult.

The Reference Level Trick

Potential energy is relative. It is zero at a chosen reference level (usually the ground). If a body goes below the reference level (like dropping into a mine shaft of depth $h$), its gravitational potential energy becomes negative: $U = -mgh$.

4 Kinetic Energy & The Work-Energy Theorem

Kinetic Energy ($K$) is the energy possessed by a body by virtue of its state of motion. If an object is moving, it has kinetic energy.

Mathematical Formula

$$ K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 $$

Kinetic energy is directly proportional to mass, but it is proportional to the square of velocity. Doubling velocity increases KE by a factor of 4.

Derivation of The Work-Energy Theorem

The work-energy theorem states that the work done by a net force on a body is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.

Mathematical Proof

Consider a body of mass $m$ moving with initial velocity $u$.

A constant retarding force $F$ opposes its motion, bringing it to rest ($v = 0$) over a displacement $S$.

Retardation $a = \frac{F}{m}$.

From the 3rd equation of kinematics:

$$ v^2 = u^2 - 2aS $$

Substitute $v = 0$:

$$ 0 = u^2 - 2aS \implies 2aS = u^2 $$

Multiply both sides by $\frac{m}{2}$:

$$ m a S = \frac{1}{2} m u^2 $$

Since Force $F = ma$:

$$ F \times S = \frac{1}{2} m u^2 $$

Thus, Work Done ($F \times S$) in stopping the body = Initial Kinetic Energy of the body.

$$ W = \Delta K = K_{final} - K_{initial} $$

5 The KE-Momentum Mathematical Relation

This is arguably the most mathematically tested concept in the ICSE syllabus for this chapter. You must master the relationship between Kinetic Energy ($K$) and Linear Momentum ($p$).

The Derivation

We know: $ p = mv \implies v = \frac{p}{m} $

We know: $ K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 $

Substitute $v$ in the KE equation:

$$ K = \frac{1}{2} m \left(\frac{p}{m}\right)^2 $$

$$ K = \frac{1}{2} m \left(\frac{p^2}{m^2}\right) $$

The Core Formulas

$$ K = \frac{p^2}{2m} $$

$$ p = \sqrt{2mK} $$

Board Exam Graph Traps

1. Graph of $K$ vs. $p$ (Mass constant): Since $K \propto p^2$, the graph is a Parabola opening upwards.

2. Graph of $p$ vs. $K$ (Mass constant): Since $p \propto \sqrt{K}$, the graph is a Parabola opening sideways.

3. Graph of $p$ vs. $\sqrt{K}$: Because $p = \sqrt{2m} \times \sqrt{K}$, this forms the equation of a straight line ($y = mx$). The graph is a Straight Line passing through the origin, and its slope equals $\sqrt{2m}$.

6 Principle of Conservation of Energy

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only change from one form to another. For an isolated system, the total mechanical energy (PE + KE) remains strictly constant throughout its motion.

Rigorous Proof: Freely Falling Body

Consider a body of mass $m$ dropped from a height $h$ above the ground. Let's calculate its total mechanical energy at three distinct points: A (top), B (middle), and C (just hitting the ground).

At Point A (Top) Height = $h$, Velocity = $0$

$K = 0$ (since at rest)

$U = mgh$

Total Energy $E_A = K + U = 0 + mgh = \mathbf{mgh}$

At Point B (Middle) Let it fall distance $x$. Height from ground = $(h-x)$.

Velocity squared ($v_1^2$) = $u^2 + 2gx = 0 + 2gx = 2gx$

$K = \frac{1}{2}mv_1^2 = \frac{1}{2}m(2gx) = \mathbf{mgx}$

$U = mg(h-x) = \mathbf{mgh - mgx}$

Total Energy $E_B = K + U = mgx + (mgh - mgx) = \mathbf{mgh}$

At Point C (Bottom) Height = $0$, fallen distance = $h$.

Velocity squared ($v^2$) = $u^2 + 2gh = 0 + 2gh = 2gh$

$K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}m(2gh) = \mathbf{mgh}$

$U = 0$ (at ground level)

Total Energy $E_C = K + U = mgh + 0 = \mathbf{mgh}$

Conclusion: $E_A = E_B = E_C = mgh$

The total mechanical energy remains entirely conserved.

Final Exam Trap Checklist

The "Percentage Change" Numerical

A very common ICSE question: "If momentum increases by 20%, find the % increase in KE."
Do not just write 20%. Since $K \propto p^2$, new momentum $p' = 1.2p$. New KE $K' \propto (1.2p)^2 = 1.44 p^2$. Thus, $K'$ is $1.44K$. The increase is $0.44K$, which is 44%. Always square the multiplier!

Work done by Centripetal Force

If asked about the work done by the gravitational pull of the Earth on a satellite, the answer is strictly Zero. The displacement (tangent) is always at $90^\circ$ to the force (radius). $\cos 90^\circ = 0$. Provide this exact mathematical reasoning.

Same KE, Different Mass. Which has more Momentum?

Let a heavy body (mass $M$) and a light body (mass $m$) have the same Kinetic Energy ($K$). Since $p = \sqrt{2mK}$, momentum $p \propto \sqrt{m}$ for a constant $K$. Therefore, the heavier body has greater momentum.
