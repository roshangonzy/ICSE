1 Lens Anatomy & Classification

A lens is a transparent refracting medium bounded by two surfaces, of which at least one is spherical. The nature of these surfaces dictates how the lens will manipulate light.

Convex (Converging) Lens

Thicker at the center and thinner at the edges. A beam of light parallel to the principal axis converges (meets) at a single point after refraction.

  • Bi-convex: Both surfaces bulge outwards.
  • Plano-convex: One surface is flat, the other bulges outwards.
  • Concavo-convex: One surface is concave, the other is convex (bulges out more strongly).

Concave (Diverging) Lens

Thinner at the center and thicker at the edges. A beam of light parallel to the principal axis diverges (spreads out) after refraction.

  • Bi-concave: Both surfaces curve inwards.
  • Plano-concave: One surface is flat, the other curves inwards.
  • Convexo-concave: One surface is convex, the other is concave (curves inward more strongly).

2 The Prism Model of Lenses

To truly understand why a lens converges or diverges light, we must mentally deconstruct it. A lens can be imagined as a stack of tiny, truncated prisms and a central rectangular glass slab.

Convex Lens as Prisms

In a convex lens, the hypothetical prisms are stacked with their bases facing the center. Because a prism always bends light towards its base, the incoming parallel rays are all bent inwards towards the central axis, resulting in convergence.

Concave Lens as Prisms

In a concave lens, the hypothetical prisms are stacked with their bases facing outwards (towards the edges). Consequently, incoming parallel rays are bent towards the outward bases, spreading away from the central axis, resulting in divergence.

The very center of both lenses acts like a flat, parallel-sided rectangular glass slab. Light passing precisely through the center emerges undeviated.

3 Essential Terminology & Principal Foci

Optical Centre ($O$)

A specific point on the principal axis of a lens such that a ray of light directed towards it passes through the lens undeviated and undisplaced. (This holds true for a thin lens, which is the scope of the ICSE syllabus).

Principal Axis

The imaginary straight line joining the centers of curvature ($C_1$ and $C_2$) of the two spherical surfaces that make up the lens. It passes straight through the Optical Centre.

The Two Principal Foci

First Principal Focus ($F_1$)

A point on the principal axis such that rays starting from it (convex lens) or appearing to meet at it (concave lens) become parallel to the principal axis after refraction.

Position: Left side (Convex), Right side (Concave)

Second Principal Focus ($F_2$) [The "Main" Focus]

A point on the principal axis where rays initially parallel to the principal axis actually converge (convex lens) or appear to diverge from (concave lens) after refraction.

Position: Right side (Convex), Left side (Concave)

ICSE Trap: "Define the focal length"

If the exam asks you to define focal length, or if you use '$f$' in a formula, it strictly refers to the Second Principal Focal Length ($f_2$)—the distance from the Optical Centre to $F_2$. Never define it based on $F_1$.

4 Standard Incident Rays for Diagrams

To accurately locate the position and determine the nature of an image, you must use the intersection of at least two of the following three standard incident rays.

  1. Rule 1: The Parallel Ray

    A ray of light incident parallel to the principal axis, after refraction, passes through the second principal focus $F_2$ (in a convex lens) or appears to diverge from the second principal focus $F_2$ (in a concave lens).

  2. Rule 2: The Optical Centre Ray

    A ray of light passing through the optical centre $O$ of a thin lens emerges undeviated and undisplaced. This is the easiest ray to draw—just use a straight ruler connecting the object point through the exact center $O$ of the lens.

  3. Rule 3: The Focal Ray

    A ray of light passing through the first principal focus $F_1$ (convex) or directed towards $F_1$ (concave) emerges parallel to the principal axis after refraction.

5 Convex Lenses: 6 Image Formations & Uses

A convex lens forms different types of images depending entirely on where the object is placed relative to its focal length ($f$). You must memorize the position, nature, size, and real-world application for all six cases.

Case 1: Object at Infinity

Application: Burning Glass / Camera Objective
Image Position At $F_2$
Size Highly Diminished (Point)
Nature Real & Inverted

Case 2: Object Beyond $2F_1$

Application: Photographic Camera Lens
Image Position Between $F_2$ and $2F_2$
Size Diminished
Nature Real & Inverted

Case 3: Object exactly at $2F_1$

Application: Terrestrial Telescope Erecting Lens
Image Position Exactly at $2F_2$
Size Same Size as Object
Nature Real & Inverted

Note: This is the only case where a real image is formed at a 1:1 scale. It simply flips the image.

Case 4: Object between $F_1$ and $2F_1$

Application: Cinema Projector / Slide Projector
Image Position Beyond $2F_2$
Size Magnified
Nature Real & Inverted

Case 5: Object exactly at $F_1$

Application: Collimator of a Spectrometer
Image Position At Infinity
Size Highly Magnified
Nature Real & Inverted

Case 6: Object between $O$ and $F_1$

Application: Magnifying Glass (Simple Microscope)
Image Position Same side as object
Size Magnified
Nature Virtual & Erect

Crucial Exception: This is the ONLY case where a convex lens forms a Virtual and Erect image. The rays must be extended backward using dotted lines to intersect.

6 Concave Lenses: The Universal Case

Unlike a convex lens, a concave lens is incredibly consistent. It acts primarily to diverge light.

The Universal Concave Rule

For any object position between the optical center ($O$) and infinity, a concave lens always forms an image that is:

1. Virtual 2. Erect 3. Diminished

The image is always formed on the same side as the object, strictly lying between the optical centre ($O$) and the second principal focus ($F_2$).

Concave Lens Applications:

  • Spectacles for Myopia: Used to correct short-sightedness by diverging incoming light before it hits the eye lens.
  • Galilean Telescope: Used as the eye-lens to obtain a final erect image.
  • Peepholes: Used in doors to provide a wide, diminished, erect field of view.

7 Cartesian Sign Convention

To mathematically solve lens numericals, we superimpose an X-Y coordinate graph over the lens, taking the Optical Centre ($O$) as the origin $(0,0)$.

Parameter Convex Lens Concave Lens
Object Distance ($u$) Always Negative ($-$) Always Negative ($-$)
Focal Length ($f$) Always Positive ($+$) Always Negative ($-$)
Real Image Dist. ($v$) Positive ($+$) Not Possible
Virtual Image Dist. ($v$) Negative ($-$) Negative ($-$)

8 The Lens Formula & Magnification

The Lens Formula

Relates focal length ($f$), object distance ($u$), and image distance ($v$). Ensure signs are applied before calculation.

$$ \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} $$

Linear Magnification ($m$)

Ratio of the height of the image ($I$) to the height of the object ($O$).

$$ m = \frac{I}{O} = \frac{v}{u} $$

Decoding the Magnification Value

Sign Analysis

If $m$ is Positive (+): Image is Virtual and Erect.

If $m$ is Negative (-): Image is Real and Inverted.

Magnitude Analysis

If $|m| > 1$: Magnified (Enlarged).

If $|m| = 1$: Same size.

If $|m| < 1$: Diminished.

9 Power of a Lens ($P$)

The power of a lens is a measure of its ability to converge (or diverge) a parallel beam of light falling on it. A thicker convex lens bends light more sharply, meaning it has a shorter focal length and a higher optical power.

If focal length is in Meters

$$ P = \frac{1}{f} $$

OR

If focal length is in cm

$$ P = \frac{100}{f} $$

SI Unit: Dioptre (D)

1 Dioptre is the power of a lens whose focal length is exactly 1 metre.

Sign matching:
Convex Lens Power = Positive ($+$)
Concave Lens Power = Negative ($-$)

Combinations of Thin Lenses

If two or more thin lenses are placed in contact, the total power ($P$) of the combination is the algebraic sum of the individual powers.

$$ P = P_1 + P_2 + P_3... $$

Final Exam Trap Checklist

Ray diagrams are notoriously strictly graded in the ICSE exams. Ensure you pass this checklist:

The Zero Mark Arrow Rule

A ray diagram without arrows showing the direction of light is considered just a geometric line. The examiner will award ZERO marks even if the drawing is perfectly accurate.

Solid vs. Dotted Lines

Real rays actually travel through space and must be drawn as solid lines. Virtual rays (rays produced backwards to meet at a virtual image) are illusions constructed by the brain, and MUST be drawn as dashed or dotted lines. Mixing these up loses marks.

The "Centimeters to Power" Numericals

If an exam question states "A convex lens has a focal length of 20 cm, find its power". Do not write $P = 1/20 = 0.05 D$. You must use $P = 100/f$ if $f$ is in cm. The correct answer is $P = 100/20 = +5.0 D$. Always include the sign for power.
