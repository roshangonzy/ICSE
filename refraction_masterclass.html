1 Lens Fundamentals & Terminology

A lens is a transparent refracting medium bounded by two surfaces, of which at least one is spherical. Lenses manipulate light to form images and are the core component of eyes, cameras, and telescopes.

Convex (Converging) Lens

Thicker at the center and thinner at the edges. It converges a parallel beam of light rays traveling parallel to its principal axis.

Concave (Diverging) Lens

Thinner at the center and thicker at the edges. It diverges a parallel beam of light rays traveling parallel to its principal axis.

Essential ICSE Definitions

  • 1. Optical Centre ($O$): A point on the principal axis of the lens such that a ray of light passing through it goes undeviated (for a thin lens).
  • 2. Principal Axis: The imaginary straight line joining the centers of curvature of the two spherical surfaces of the lens.
  • 3. First Principal Focus ($F_1$): A point on the principal axis such that rays of light starting from it (or directed towards it) become parallel to the principal axis after refraction.
  • 4. Second Principal Focus ($F_2$): A point on the principal axis where rays of light initially parallel to the principal axis actually meet (convex) or appear to diverge from (concave) after refraction.

Key Insight: Which Focus is the "Main" Focus?

When we talk about the "focus" or "focal length" of a lens in numericals, we are strictly referring to the Second Principal Focus ($F_2$). For a convex lens, $F_2$ is real and lies on the right. For a concave lens, $F_2$ is virtual and lies on the left.

2 Rules for Drawing Ray Diagrams

To accurately locate the position and determine the nature of an image formed by a lens, you must use at least two of the following three standard incident rays.

  1. Rule 1: The Parallel Ray A ray of light incident parallel to the principal axis, after refraction, passes through the second principal focus $F_2$ (in a convex lens) or appears to diverge from the second principal focus $F_2$ (in a concave lens).
  2. Rule 2: The Optical Centre Ray A ray of light passing through the optical centre $O$ of a thin lens emerges undeviated and undisplaced. (This is the easiest ray to draw—just use a straight ruler through the center point!).
  3. Rule 3: The Focal Ray A ray of light passing through the first principal focus $F_1$ (convex) or directed towards $F_1$ (concave) emerges parallel to the principal axis after refraction.

3 Image Formation by Convex Lenses

A convex lens forms different types of images depending entirely on where the object is placed relative to its focal length ($f$). There are six distinct cases you must memorize for the ICSE board exam.

Position of Object Position of Image Size of Image Nature of Image
1. At Infinity At focus $F_2$ Highly Diminished (Point) Real and Inverted
2. Beyond $2F_1$ Between $F_2$ and $2F_2$ Diminished Real and Inverted
3. At $2F_1$ At $2F_2$ Same Size Real and Inverted
4. Between $F_1$ and $2F_1$ Beyond $2F_2$ Magnified Real and Inverted
5. At $F_1$ At Infinity Highly Magnified Real and Inverted
6. Between $O$ and $F_1$ Same side as object Magnified Virtual and Erect

Application Highlights:

  • Case 3 (At 2F): Used in terrestrial telescopes as an erecting lens. It inverts an inverted image without changing its size.
  • Case 4 (Between F and 2F): Used in cinema projectors and slide projectors.
  • Case 6 (Between O and F): This is the principle behind a Magnifying Glass (Simple Microscope). The object must be closer than the focal length to see a magnified, upright image.

4 Image Formation by Concave Lenses

Unlike a convex lens, a concave lens is incredibly consistent. No matter where you place an object in front of a concave lens (except at infinity), the result is fundamentally the same.

The Universal Concave Rule

For any object position between optical center ($O$) and infinity, a concave lens always forms an image that is:

1. Virtual 2. Erect 3. Diminished

The image is always formed on the same side as the object, between the optical centre ($O$) and the focus ($F_2$).

Application: Concave lenses are primarily used in spectacles to correct Myopia (Short-sightedness) and as the eye-lens in a Galilean telescope.

5 Sign Convention & The Lens Formula

To solve numerical problems correctly, we use the Cartesian Sign Convention. Imagine the optical centre ($O$) as the origin $(0,0)$ of a graph, and the principal axis as the X-axis.

  • All distances are measured from the optical centre ($O$).
  • Distances measured in the direction of incident light (usually to the right) are taken as Positive ($+$).
  • Distances measured opposite to the incident light (usually to the left) are taken as Negative ($-$).
  • Heights above the principal axis are Positive ($+$) (Erect objects/images).
  • Heights below the principal axis are Negative ($-$) (Inverted images).

Crucial Sign Memorization

Object Distance ($u$)

Always Negative ($-$) for both lenses.

Focal Length ($f$)

Convex: Positive ($+$)
Concave: Negative ($-$)

The Lens Formula

Relates focal length ($f$), object distance ($u$), and image distance ($v$).

$$ \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} $$

6 Linear Magnification ($m$)

Linear magnification tells us how large or small the image is compared to the object. It is the ratio of the height of the image ($I$) to the height of the object ($O$).

Height Formula

$$ m = \frac{I}{O} $$

Distance Formula

$$ m = \frac{v}{u} $$

Decoding the value of $m$:

  • If $m$ is Positive: The image is Virtual and Erect.
  • If $m$ is Negative: The image is Real and Inverted.
  • If $|m| > 1$: Image is Magnified.
  • If $|m| < 1$: Image is Diminished.

7 Power of a Lens ($P$)

The power of a lens is a measure of its ability to converge (or diverge) a parallel beam of light falling on it. A thicker convex lens converges light more sharply, meaning it has a shorter focal length and a higher power.

Mathematically, it is the reciprocal of the focal length, provided the focal length is expressed in meters.

$$ P = \frac{1}{f \text{ (in meters)}} $$

OR

$$ P = \frac{100}{f \text{ (in cm)}} $$

SI Unit

The SI unit of power is Dioptre (D).
1 Dioptre is the power of a lens whose focal length is exactly 1 metre.

Sign of Power

Since $f$ is positive for Convex lenses, Power is Positive ($+$).
Since $f$ is negative for Concave lenses, Power is Negative ($-$).

Final Exam Checklist

Before walking into the ICSE physics examination, ensure you never make these common ray-diagram mistakes:

The Deadly Arrow Rule

We repeat this because it's the #1 reason students lose marks. Draw arrows on both incident and refracted rays. No arrows = Zero marks in ICSE.

Virtual Rays MUST be Dotted

If a ray is produced backwards (to form a virtual image), it must be drawn as a dashed/dotted line. Solid lines produced backward will be penalized.

Symmetry of Foci

When drawing a ray diagram, ensure the distance from Optical Centre ($O$) to $F_1$ is exactly equal to the distance from $O$ to $F_2$. Use a ruler!

Power Conversion Trap

In numericals, examiners often give focal length in centimeters. You MUST convert it to meters before using $P = 1/f$, or simply use $P = 100/f(\text{in cm})$. Forgetting this yields the wrong answer.
