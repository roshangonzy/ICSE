1 Fundamentals of Refraction

Before diving into formulas, we must understand why light bends. Light travels in a straight line as long as it remains in the same homogeneous medium. However, when it travels from one transparent medium to another, it experiences a change in speed.

Official ICSE Definition

Refraction is the change in the direction of the path of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another transparent medium of different optical density.

The Cause of Refraction

The fundamental cause of refraction is the change in the speed of light in different media. A medium in which the speed of light is more is called an optically rarer medium (e.g., air). A medium in which the speed of light is less is called an optically denser medium (e.g., glass, water).

Direction of Bending Rules:

  • Rarer to Denser: Speed decreases. The ray bends towards the normal. ($i > r$)
  • Denser to Rarer: Speed increases. The ray bends away from the normal. ($r > i$)
  • Normal Incidence: If light strikes the surface at $90^\circ$ (along the normal), angle of incidence $i = 0^\circ$. The light passes undeviated ($r = 0^\circ$), though its speed does change.

Key Insight: Optical vs. Mass Density

Do not confuse "optical density" with "mass density". Kerosene floats on water, meaning its mass density is lower than water. However, the refractive index of Kerosene ($1.44$) is higher than water ($1.33$). Therefore, Kerosene is optically denser but mass-wise rarer than water. ICSE loves asking this distinction!

2 Laws of Refraction & Snell's Law

When light undergoes refraction at a plane surface, it strictly obeys two fundamental laws.

The Two Laws

  1. The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal at the point of incidence, all lie in the same plane.
  2. Snell's Law: The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence ($i$) to the sine of the angle of refraction ($r$) is constant for a given pair of media and a given color (wavelength) of light.

Mathematical Expression of Snell's Law

$$ \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \text{constant} $$

$$ \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = _{1}\mu_{2} $$

Where $ _{1}\mu_{2} $ is the relative refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium.

3 The Refractive Index In-Depth

The Refractive Index ($\mu$) is a measure of how much a medium slows down the speed of light. Because it is a ratio of two similar quantities (speeds), it has no unit.

1. Absolute Refractive Index

When light travels from vacuum (or air) into another medium, the constant is called the absolute refractive index of that medium.

$$ \mu = \frac{\text{Speed of light in vacuum/air } (c)}{\text{Speed of light in the medium } (v)} $$

Since $c$ is the maximum possible speed in the universe ($3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$), the absolute refractive index of any transparent medium is always greater than 1.

2. Relative Refractive Index & Principle of Reversibility

When light travels from Medium 1 to Medium 2 (neither being vacuum), we use the relative refractive index. The refractive index of Medium 2 with respect to Medium 1 is written as $ _{1}\mu_{2} $.

$$ _{1}\mu_{2} = \frac{\text{Speed of light in medium 1 } (v_1)}{\text{Speed of light in medium 2 } (v_2)} $$

Relationship with Absolute Refractive Indices:

$$ _{1}\mu_{2} = \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} $$

The Principle of Reversibility of Light

If the path of a ray of light is reversed, it retraces its path exactly. Mathematically:

$$ _{1}\mu_{2} = \frac{1}{_{2}\mu_{1}} $$

Example: If the refractive index of glass with respect to air ($ _{a}\mu_{g} $) is $3/2$, then the refractive index of air with respect to glass ($ _{g}\mu_{a} $) is $2/3$.

Factors Affecting Refractive Index

The refractive index of a medium is not an absolute constant; it depends on several physical parameters. ICSE Board frequently asks for "two factors".

1. Temperature

As temperature increases, the speed of light in the medium increases. Therefore, the refractive index decreases with an increase in temperature.

2. Color / Wavelength

Speed of red light is maximum and violet is minimum in glass. Hence, $\mu$ is maximum for Violet and minimum for Red light.

3. Nature of Medium

Depends on the optical density. A denser medium (like diamond) slows light down more, resulting in a higher $\mu$ ($2.42$) compared to glass ($1.5$).

ICSE Deadly Trap: Frequency vs. Wavelength

When light travels from air to glass, its velocity ($v$) decreases and its wavelength ($\lambda$) decreases proportionately ($v = f \times \lambda$).

However, the FREQUENCY of light DOES NOT CHANGE during refraction.

Why? Because frequency is a characteristic of the source emitting the light, not the medium it travels through. Writing "frequency changes" guarantees a zero for that question.

4 Refraction through a Glass Slab

When a ray of light passes through a rectangular glass slab with parallel faces, it undergoes refraction twice: once at the air-glass interface (bending towards the normal) and once at the glass-air interface (bending away from the normal).

Key Characteristics:

  • The angle of incidence ($i$) is equal to the angle of emergence ($e$). ($i = e$)
  • The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray.
  • There is no angular deviation, but there is a lateral shift.

What is Lateral Displacement?

The perpendicular distance between the path of the original incident ray and the emergent ray is called lateral displacement.

Factors increasing Lateral Displacement:

  1. Thickness of the block: More thickness = greater displacement.
  2. Angle of incidence ($i$): Greater angle of incidence = greater displacement.
  3. Refractive Index ($\mu$): Higher $\mu$ = greater displacement (Hence, violet light is displaced more than red light).

5 Real & Apparent Depth

An object placed in a denser medium (like a coin at the bottom of a pool of water), when viewed from a rarer medium (like air), appears to be at a lesser depth than its actual depth. This optical illusion is a direct consequence of refraction.

As light rays originating from the coin travel from water to air, they bend away from the normal. When these diverged rays reach the human eye, the brain traces them back in straight lines, forming a virtual image at a higher position.

Depth Formula

$$ \mu = \frac{\text{Real Depth}}{\text{Apparent Depth}} $$

Shift Formula

Shift = Real Depth - Apparent Depth

$$ \text{Shift} = \text{Real Depth} \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{\mu}\right) $$

Consequences of this phenomenon in daily life:

  • A stick partially immersed in water appears bent or broken at the surface.
  • A pool of water appears shallower than it actually is (Dangerous trap for swimmers!).
  • Letters printed on a page appear raised when viewed through a thick glass block.
  • Early sunrise and delayed sunset: The sun is visible a few minutes before actual sunrise because atmospheric refraction bends the light from below the horizon.
Highest Weightage Topic

Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

What happens if light traveling from a denser to a rarer medium hits the boundary at a very large angle? It gets completely reflected back into the denser medium. No light is refracted. This phenomenon is Total Internal Reflection.

Case 1: $i < C$

Ray is weakly reflected and strongly refracted into the rarer medium. It bends away from the normal.

Case 2: $i = C$

The refracted ray grazes the surface of separation. Angle of refraction $r = 90^\circ$. This angle of incidence is the Critical Angle.

Case 3: $i > C$

No refracted ray exists. The ray is completely reflected back into the denser medium according to the laws of reflection. TIR occurs.

The Critical Angle ($C$)

The angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is exactly $90^\circ$.

Formula

$$ \sin C = \frac{1}{\mu} $$

Glass ($_{a}\mu_{g} = 1.5$) $C \approx 42^\circ$
Water ($_{a}\mu_{w} = 1.33$) $C \approx 49^\circ$
Diamond ($_{a}\mu_{d} = 2.42$) $C \approx 24^\circ$

Essential Conditions for TIR

  1. The light must travel from a denser medium to a rarer medium.
  2. The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle for that specific pair of media ($i > C$).

7 Applications of TIR in Prisms

A right-angled isosceles glass prism (angles $90^\circ, 45^\circ, 45^\circ$) is extensively used in optical instruments to alter the path of light by taking advantage of TIR. Since the critical angle for glass is $\approx 42^\circ$, any ray striking inside the glass at $45^\circ$ will suffer Total Internal Reflection ($45^\circ > 42^\circ$).

Advantages of a Prism over a Silvered Plane Mirror:
A silvered mirror absorbs some light (about 10-20%) and suffers from multiple reflections (ghost images) if the glass is thick. A prism utilizing TIR reflects 100% of the light, yielding a brighter and perfectly distinct image.

1. Deviation by $90^\circ$

Light enters normally to one of the sides containing the right angle. It strikes the hypotenuse at $45^\circ$. Since $45^\circ > 42^\circ$, it undergoes TIR and emerges normally from the other side.

Application: Periscope

2. Deviation by $180^\circ$

Light enters normally through the hypotenuse. It strikes the first perpendicular side at $45^\circ$, undergoes TIR, strikes the second perpendicular side at $45^\circ$, undergoes TIR again, and emerges anti-parallel.

Application: Binoculars

3. Erecting Prism

Light enters parallel to the hypotenuse, refracts, hits the hypotenuse internally at $>42^\circ$, undergoes TIR, and refracts out. It inverts the image without any deviation in the overall path.

Application: Slide Projector

Final Exam Checklist

Before walking into the ICSE physics examination, ensure you never make these common mistakes:

The Arrow Rule

A ray diagram without arrows showing the direction of light is considered just a geometric line. The ICSE examiner will award ZERO marks even if the drawing and angles are perfectly accurate.

Drawing the Normal

Always draw the normal as a dotted or dashed line at the exact point of incidence using a protractor (or very carefully freehand at a strict 90 degree angle to the surface).

Distinguishing Deviation vs Shift

In a prism, the ray deviates by an angle. In a rectangular slab, the ray shifts laterally by a distance, but emerges perfectly parallel to its original path (zero angular deviation).

Handling Multiple Media Numericals

If light goes from air $\rightarrow$ water $\rightarrow$ glass, remember the chaining rule: $$ _{a}\mu_{w} \times _{w}\mu_{g} \times _{g}\mu_{a} = 1 $$ This implies $ _{w}\mu_{g} = \frac{_{a}\mu_{g}}{_{a}\mu_{w}} $.
